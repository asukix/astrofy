## "Run Forrest Run"

Today’s world is all about speed, encouraging us to want everything quickly and to get things done fast. Nowadays, we can obtain almost anything—from stores or online—and most of it arrives very quickly. A piece of fruit or an item of clothing from the other side of the globe can be in our hands in no time. Thanks to modern search engines and AI, we also get answers almost instantly. The quote in the header is about this rush, too—isn't it?

## Run or walk?

In my childhood, I learned that getting things done quickly was a good thing. As an adult entering the workforce, I consistently saw that this approach often led to sloppy work—yet the habit of rushing to finish tasks was already deeply ingrained. While learning to develop my skills, I observed those around me who were highly regarded and skilled. I began to understand their way of thinking and adopted their working methods. Gradually, I shifted my focus from speed to quality. The result was that my work not only improved in quality but, more and more often, actually took less time to complete.

## The thinking space

In 2025, I began to consciously develop my mindset. I learned that by giving my thoughts time and space, I gain a deeper understanding, the quality of my work improves, and I become more effective. Over time, the urge to rush has transformed into a preference for pausing to think. I’d like to believe this is the right path, though I can’t say for sure. One thing is certain: I **enjoy** it. And come to think of it, the quote in the first header mentions Forrest Gump running—but he wasn’t rushing; he simply enjoyed the act itself. And that allowed him to step back from the world and "pause to think."

## My mental model

I enjoy sitting down to think about various topics, and I like doing so even more while walking. I’ve found that if I have a foundational understanding of a subject and spend a lot of time thinking about it, I develop my own mental model—one that stems from that understanding. When I grasp something at a deeper level, I’m able to construct the underlying system in my mind on my own. The first topic for which this happened was Swift’s "copy-on-write" mechanism. The best part is that when I explain it to someone else—or simply rethink it myself—I often notice details I hadn’t seen before, which frequently leads me to a better answer.

## Destruction of a mental model

There is one aspect of building mental models that I find difficult: dismantling my own mental model. How I go about doing this—and why it’s beneficial—is a topic for another article.

## Recap in one sentence

When I pause and create my own mental model during learning, I gain a deeper understanding that I can draw on at any time.
